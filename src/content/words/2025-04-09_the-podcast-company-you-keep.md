Since going from fully remote to a hybrid work schedule a few years ago, I've found myself spending a lot more time in the car. I fill the drive time commute like anyone else would, a few tunes streaming from my phone, a little NPR here and there, chapters from an audiobook every so often, and of course, a podcast or two when I'm feeling it.

Podcasts have always been a big deal to me. I've been immersed in them ever since the first iPod (not that iPods were the only way to listen, but that's honestly where the term emerged from), and I expect this won't be the last time I mention podcasts on this blog.

But podcasts have taken on a new life in the last decade, even more so since the last election. In the days when you had to attach an FM transmitter to your portable music player in order to listen in the car, no one could have ever imagined how the content creator universe would evolve into its current state.

Certainly there are many fantastic people producing many fantastic media things on the internet. Podcasts are just one facet of the new media ecosystem, but one sociological phenomenon that's become more and more apparent are the ways in which viewers and listeners find fellowship with the content they consume.

The parasocial bonds that form between podcasters and their audiences are well documented. There's an authenticity to the medium that's hard to find in other places and many listeners compare listening to their favorite shows as like being in a room with the hosts, having been invited to a cool party where they're introduced to other thought-provoking and interesting individuals.

My feeling is that not only do people feel drawn to certain shows and certain personalities, but it's also possible to gain a lot of information about a person based on the podcasts they like to listen to. If the analogy of a podcast host being like someone's friend is true, and if people are shaped by the company they keep, then it follows that the internet personalities one appreciates really do influence a person's way of looking at the world, and as well their thoughts, feelings, and actions.

As podcasts fill our offline void previously filled by friends and real life relationships, this fact becomes even more relevant and hard to turn away from.

But also, people tend to be complex and I don't want to imply we're empty vessels searching for the first soothing voice that will confirm our biases about the world. There are many podcasts I listen to that I find compelling but also end up having significant disagreements with the host's point of view over (Sam Harris I'm looking at you). Some are also a popcorn listen. I would not want someone to judge me or assume my opinions for enjoying Steve-O's Wild Ride.

I think the real test is to understand how deeply a person trusts or sides with their favorite online personalities and content creators. Not that critical thinking is ever in endless supply, but no matter who you pay attention to across the entire spectrum of personalities (authors, politicians, religious leaders, and yes, TikTok influencers), if you unflaggingly agree with everything that person says and find yourself being drawn deeper and deeper into their (likely contrarian) worldview, you might honestly be in the throws of your own parasocial bond.

Friends don't let friends slip into unhealthy relationships.

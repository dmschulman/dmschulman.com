When is a blog not a blog?

When it's a website!

Things were simpler back in the Wordpress days. You had “posts” and you had “pages”. Posts were where you did your chronologically-sorted single page blogging and Pages were where you did your did everything else. This paradigm was great for the majority of Wordpress users but what if you wanted to do something more?

Wordpress plugins could help there and gave users easy access to building photo galleries or putting together a list of your favorite books, but plugins were a messy crutch and likely ended up harming more WP sites than they helped. If you knew PHP and SQL you could theoretically build these features yourself but this was also messy in practice and collided with Wordpress's value proposition of being fast and easy to use. Many of us moved on.

Enter “the collection", a content type that can wrap around a set of items and treat them as their own thing. A third content shape emerges! Jekyll was the first framework where I encountered this way of organizing content and I suddenly found everything I wanted to do outside of simple blogging boiled down to a collection. Defining schema for a set of information and the being able to use that structure in various ways is powerful!

The collection shape solves maybe 80% of my needs as a dev working on my personal website, but as the web evolves and as we find new ways to express information, new shapes and organizational structures continue to emerge.

One content shape that's been on my radar is the “[digital](https://dmschulman.com/words/2025/the-metaphor-of-a-digital-garden/) [garden](https://dmschulman.com/words/2025/blogging-is-digital-gardening/)”, or what I think is fair to think of as a personal [wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiki) that does away with the rigid hierarchies and backbone that make Wikipedia what it is. While I'm still identifying how I want to structure my version of a digital garden I believe the content shape of a digital garden can solve some of the annoyances the post/page paradigm foisted on bloggers way back when.

I want to write a lot about how I built this website, everything from the basic structure and tips I found useful to how I developed many of my collections. In the old days that might have been a series of blog posts, many items of content you're meant to browse in sequence (a linear shape) that might share a category or have links going between items in the series. Still a useful way to do things but writing full tutorials can be time consuming and my mind likes to jump around to different topics so focusing on a series can be difficult (not to mention the reader's attention span ain't what it used to be).

Though blog posts like this one represent a self-contained thought written out at a specific point in time, digital gardens provide a scaffold for taking similar self-contained thoughts and building an association between them, which in turn allows you to both better organize your content and also grow that repository into a kind of knowledge base. Think about documentation for a framework or library. You don't start on page 1 and go to page 20 to find how to define a certain kind of value, you just land on the page that has what you're looking for thanks to search.

Navigation across a series or collection is just as important to the experience of the content itself. Whether it's a linear sequence, a hub-and-spoke model, a hierarchical document, or something else the way the user moves through it is something to consider.

While the general structure of a digital garden has [become somewhat defined](https://maggieappleton.com/garden-history) I think the jury is still out on the best way a user can experience everything as this content shape is, by definition, somewhat messy and scattershot. The name even implies constant tending and care is necessary to grow the resource into a useful thing. I'll continue to tinker and find the right mold for the experience I want to present but recognizing the fact that a content series is and can be more than just a flat series of chronological posts is helpful and gives perspective into just how far we've come with utilizing the web's core basic principals.
